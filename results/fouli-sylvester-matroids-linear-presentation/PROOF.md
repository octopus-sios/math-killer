# A quadratic Sylvester matroid with a nonlinear first syzygy

**Counterexample to Conjecture 4.1** of Fouli, Grate, Kara, LaClair, McCullough, Nguyen, Sobieska and Udanshive, [*Graded Betti numbers of graded Möbius algebras of uniform matroids*](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03827v1#S4). There is a simple rank-three matroid on 15 points in which every pair belongs to a three-element circuit, yet its quadratic graded Möbius algebra over $\mathbb Q$ has $\beta_{2,4}=20$. A second 15-point example has every line of size three and $\beta_{2,4}=4$. Only the Sylvester-to-linear-presentation implication fails. Proposition 4.4 of the paper proves the other direction.

## The matroid

Let $V=\mathbb F_2^3$. Use one affine point for each $x\in V$ and a disjoint set of direction points $D=\{d_h:0\ne h\in V\}$. Declare the lines to be

$$
D,\qquad \{x,y,d_{x+y}\}\quad(x,y\in V,\ x\ne y).
$$

Every pair determines exactly one line. For a mixed pair $x,d_h$, the second affine point is $x+h$. Declare all sets of size at most two independent, all noncollinear triples independent, and no larger sets independent. For the only nontrivial augmentation check, a noncollinear triple cannot be contained in the line through an independent pair. This gives a simple rank-three matroid $M$, and the displayed lines are precisely its rank-two flats. They have sizes seven and three, so $M$ is Sylvester. This uses the definition in Section 4: existence of a three-element circuit, not uniqueness. The paper explicitly includes every uniform matroid of rank two on at least three points. Unlike the truncation in Example 4.3 of the paper, which retains the seven binary triples among the direction points, here these triples are replaced by a single seven-point line. Throughout, the coefficient field of the algebra is $\mathbb Q$; the binary vector space only indexes the incidence structure.

## Quadratic generation

Write $A=\mathsf{GMA}(M)$ and map $S=\mathbb Q[X_e:e\in E]$ to $A$ by $X_e\mapsto y_e$. Let $J$ be generated by the squares $X_e^2$ and the differences of pair products belonging to the same line (each pair consists of two distinct points). The flat multiplication rule gives $J\subseteq I_M$, and $S/J\to A$ is an isomorphism in degrees zero through two: in degree two there is one surviving class for each line.

Modulo $J$, one may replace any two distinct factors by any distinct pair on their line. A cubic with a repeated factor is zero; a cubic on three distinct collinear points is also zero, since replacing two factors can duplicate the third. All remaining cubic monomials are equal:

1. With two directions and one affine point $x$, one may change the pair of directions arbitrarily on $D$. To change $x$ to $y\ne x$, first include $d_{x+y}$ in this pair, and replace $x,d_{x+y}$ by $y,d_{x+y}$. Thus all such cubics agree.
2. With one direction $d$ and two affine points $x,y$, noncollinearity gives $d\ne d_{x+y}$. Replace $x,y$ by $x,d_{x+y}$ to obtain the first case.
3. With three affine points $x,y,z$, replace $x,y$ by $x,d_{x+y}$. The new triple is noncollinear because $x+z\ne x+y$, and falls under the preceding case.

The common class maps to the nonzero top flat in $A$. Each variable occurs in some noncollinear triple, so the common class times *any* variable is zero: choose a representative containing that variable and use its square. This kills every quartic, hence every higher degree. The surjection $S/J\to A$ is an isomorphism in every degree, proving $I_M=J$.

## A separating functional in the Koszul complex

Use $K_i=A\otimes\bigwedge^i \mathbb Q^E$ with differential

$$
\partial(y\otimes e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_s})
=\sum_{k=1}^s(-1)^{k-1}y\,y_{i_k}\otimes
 e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{e_{i_k}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_s}.
$$

Label the affine points $0,\ldots,7$ in binary, with addition by bitwise XOR. The affine subset $U=\{0,1,2,3\}$ and its directions $d_1,d_2,d_3$ form a seven-point set $T$. In the restriction $M|T$, six lines are the triples on pairs of elements of $U$; the seventh line is $D_T=\{d_1,d_2,d_3\}$. Only this seventh line is enlarged in $M$.

On the internal-degree-four piece of $K_2$ define the functional $\Phi$ on a basis expression $y_L\otimes e_i\wedge e_j$ by

$$
\Phi(L;i,j)=
\mathbf 1_{T\setminus L}(i)\mathbf 1_U(j)
-\mathbf 1_{T\setminus L}(j)\mathbf 1_U(i)
$$

if $L$ is one of the six unchanged lines, and zero on every other line. The formula is alternating.

**It kills boundaries.** In the Koszul complex of the restriction, give $y_T\otimes e_j$ the value $\mathbf 1_U(j)$ to define a functional $g$ on $[K_1]_4$. Since a point multiplies a line to the top flat exactly when it is off the line, $g\partial_2$ has the displayed formula on each of the six lines. It is zero on $D_T$, since $T\setminus D_T=U$. Thus $g\partial_2$ agrees with $\Phi$ on all terms from the restriction, with $D_T$ replaced by $D$.

Consider a boundary of $y_t\otimes e_a\wedge e_b\wedge e_c$ in $M$. If $t,a,b,c$ all belong to $T$, its pairing with $\Phi$ is $g\partial_2\partial_3=0$ computed in the restriction: equal-point products are zero in both matroids and the only enlarged pair-line contributes zero in both. If any index is outside $T$, every term pairs to zero. Indeed, a nonzero term would require the two multiplying points to lie on one of the six unchanged lines and both remaining wedge indices to belong to $T$. This checks all boundaries in degree four.

**It detects a cycle.** Take $L=\{0,1,d_1\}$ and $L'=\{4,5,d_1\}$. Both $d_2$ and the affine point $2$ are outside both lines, so multiplying either line by either point yields the same top flat $y_E$. Hence

$$
z=(y_L-y_{L'})\otimes e_{d_2}\wedge e_2
$$

is a cycle. Its pairing is $\Phi(z)=1$: the $L$ term contributes one, and $L'$ is not an unchanged line of the restriction. Therefore $z$ is not a boundary. The resulting nonzero class in $H_2(K)_4=\operatorname{Tor}_2^S(A,\mathbb Q)_4$ proves that $I_M$ is not linearly presented.

The construction works without change with $V=\mathbb F_2^m$ for any $m\ge 3$: keep $U$ a two-dimensional subspace, and take $L'$ on two points of one of its other cosets with difference $1$. The argument only uses the unchanged six lines, their enlarged direction line, and the quadratic pair moves above. In particular this gives counterexamples of unbounded size, all of rank three.

There is in fact one independent obstruction for every affine two-dimensional subspace $W$ of $V$. To see this, use $W$ in place of $U$ and its three directions to construct $\Phi_W$ as above. Write $W=x+\langle h,k\rangle$ with $h,k$ independent. Put $w=x+k$, and choose $x'\notin W$. Form $z_W$ using the lines on $x,x+h$ and $x',x'+h$ in that order, and the wedge $e_{d_k}\wedge e_w$. Both lines miss $w$ and $d_k$, so this is a cycle. For $\Phi_Y$ to pair nontrivially with either term, the affine two-dimensional subspace $Y$ must contain that term's affine endpoints and $w$, and its direction space must contain $k$. Since the endpoints have difference $h$, the direction space must be $\langle h,k\rangle$. The first line selects $Y=W$ and contributes one; the second selects a disjoint coset, which cannot contain $w$. The pairing matrix is the identity. Counting ordered independent pairs $h,k$, six ordered bases per two-dimensional direction space, and the cosets of each gives

$$
\beta_{2,4}\ \ge\ 2^{m-2}\frac{(2^m-1)(2^m-2)}{6}.
$$

For the 15-point example this lower bound is 14. Direct integer elimination over $\mathbb Q$ in the verifier gives the exact value **20**: the middle space has dimension $29\binom{15}{2}=3045$, and the outgoing and incoming differentials have ranks $15$ and $3010$, respectively.


## A counterexample with every line of size three

The introduction uses the word *unique* for the circuit through a pair, whereas Section 4 only requires existence. Even imposing uniqueness does not restore the conjecture. On points $0,\ldots,14$ take the following 35 triples as lines:

```
(0, 1, 2), (0, 3, 11), (0, 4, 12), (0, 5, 6), (0, 7, 8)
(0, 9, 13), (0, 10, 14), (1, 3, 5), (1, 4, 6), (1, 7, 9)
(1, 8, 10), (1, 11, 13), (1, 12, 14), (2, 3, 8), (2, 4, 11)
(2, 5, 14), (2, 6, 9), (2, 7, 10), (2, 12, 13), (3, 4, 7)
(3, 6, 12), (3, 9, 10), (3, 13, 14), (4, 5, 9), (4, 8, 14)
(4, 10, 13), (5, 7, 13), (5, 8, 11), (5, 10, 12), (6, 7, 14)
(6, 8, 13), (6, 10, 11), (7, 11, 12), (8, 9, 12), (9, 11, 14)
```

The finite checks for this example are included in `verify.py`. Each of the 105 pairs occurs exactly once; the same rank-three augmentation argument therefore applies. To test quadraticity, form the degree-$k$ graph on sorted monomials, with an edge for replacing two factors by another pair on their line. Quotienting by the quadratic binomials identifies each connected component to one vector. Killing the squares kills exactly the components containing repeated-factor monomials. For $k=0,1,2,3,4$ the surviving component counts are $1,15,35,1,0$. They agree with the flat counts, and vanishing in degree four forces vanishing in all higher degrees, proving quadratic generation.

There is also a unit pairing for this example. Use $U'=\{1,6,10,13\}$ and $A'=\{4,8,11\}$, with $T'=U'\cup A'$. The six lines on pairs from $U'$ lie entirely in $T'$. Together with the *formal* line $A'$, they form a reference Fano plane. Here $A'$ is not a line of the actual matroid. Nevertheless, the functional above, supported on the six unchanged lines and using $U'$ in place of $U$, still kills every boundary. Indeed every pair in $A'$ gives value zero both on its actual line and on the formal line; every other pair in $T'$ has the same line as in the reference plane. An outside index cannot contribute, exactly as before. The cycle on the difference of the lines $\{1,4,6\}$ and $\{0,1,2\}$ with wedge $e_8\wedge e_{10}$ has pairing one. Thus nonvanishing in this example also has an explicit certificate independent of rank elimination.

In the degree-four Koszul complex the middle dimension is $35\binom{15}{2}=3675$. The verifier constructs both signed differentials from the pair-to-line table and computes their ranks by integer elimination: cancel a leading coefficient against a stored pivot, then divide the result by its content. These operations preserve the echelon rank over $\mathbb Q$. The outgoing rank is 15 and the incoming rank is 3656, giving $\beta_{2,4}=3675-15-3656=4$. This second example does not rely on the seven-point direction line of the first.


## Reproduction

Run `python3 verify.py` in this directory. It builds the 15-point incidence structure, checks the degree-three pair moves, and tests the cycle and functional against the entire relevant Koszul differential using integer arithmetic. A deliberate change to one coefficient of the functional must be rejected. No matrix-rank estimate or positive-characteristic inference is needed.

The Lean module `FouliSylvesterMatroids` encodes the same 15-point multiplication tables and proves over the rationals that the displayed cycle is closed and is not any finite linear combination of third-differential columns. Matroid augmentation, quadratic generation and the infinite-family argument above are not formalized there.
